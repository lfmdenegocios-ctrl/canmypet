Can I give my pet this human medicine?

Straight answers on the medicines people actually reach for — plus the packet traps that turn a "usually fine" drug into an emergency.

We deliberately don't publish doses. Not because we're being cagey — because a safe dose depends on your pet's weight, age, kidney and liver function, and everything else they take. Getting that wrong is exactly how these medicines cause harm. Your vet will give you a number in a two-minute phone call, and most clinics won't charge for the question.

Covers the human medicines most often given to pets or swallowed by accident. It is information, not veterinary advice, and it can't account for your pet's individual health. If your pet has already taken something, call rather than read.

Questions people ask

Why won't you just tell me the Benadryl dose?

Because the number on its own is the least important part, and publishing it invites the two mistakes that actually hurt dogs: using a combination product (the "-D", "Plus Congestion" or "Severe Cold" versions contain decongestants that are genuinely toxic), and giving it to a pet whose heart, liver, kidneys or other medications make it a bad idea. A vet checks all of that in the same call where they give you the number.

What painkiller can I give my dog at home?

None from your own cabinet. This is the single most common well-meant mistake in pet medicine. Dogs have their own anti-inflammatories — carprofen, meloxicam, grapiprant and others — that are dosed by weight and monitored. They are not "stronger ibuprofen"; they are drugs a dog's body can actually process. If your pet is in pain, that is itself a reason to be seen.

My pet already swallowed something — what now?

Call now rather than waiting for symptoms: ASPCA Animal Poison Control 888-426-4435 or Pet Poison Helpline 855-764-7661. Have the packet in your hand — the strength in milligrams, how many are missing, and your pet's weight are the three things they will ask for. Don't induce vomiting unless they tell you to.

Are "natural" or herbal remedies safer?

Not automatically. Many essential oils are toxic to cats, garlic supplements damage red blood cells, and CBD products vary wildly in what they actually contain. Natural describes the origin, not the safety margin. Run anything past your vet the same way you would a pharmacy medicine.

How do I stop my pet getting into medication?

Most poisonings are dropped pills and handbags left on the floor. Take tablets over a sink or a closed surface rather than above carpet, keep bags and bedside drawers shut, and never store pet and human medication in the same place — dosing mix-ups happen more often than people admit.
