Dog choking & pet CPR — step by step

Answer two questions and get only the steps that apply to your pet right now. Built to be usable one-handed, in a panic, on a phone.

🚑 Get someone driving to a vet now

If anyone else is with you, send them to start the car and call the clinic while you work. First aid buys minutes — it is not a substitute for a vet. Even a pet that recovers completely needs checking, because CPR can crack ribs and an inhaled object can cause pneumonia hours later.

Questions people ask

How do I know it's really choking?

A choking pet is in visible panic: pawing frantically at the mouth, retching with nothing coming up, making little or no sound (or a high-pitched wheeze), with gums and tongue turning blue or grey. Loud coughing is different — noise means air is still moving. And reverse sneezing, a honking snorting fit common in small breeds, looks alarming but resolves by itself within about a minute.

Can I hurt my pet doing CPR?

Yes, and you should do it anyway when it is needed. Rib fractures happen even when trained professionals do compressions. The alternative to imperfect CPR on a pet with no heartbeat is not a better outcome — it is no outcome. What you must not do is practise compressions on a conscious animal or one that is still breathing.

Does CPR actually work on pets?

Be prepared for the honest answer: survival to discharge after cardiac arrest is low, in the region of a few percent, and better when arrest happens in a hospital. That is not a reason to skip it — early bystander CPR measurably improves the odds and keeps the brain supplied while you travel. But nobody should feel they failed if it does not work.

What are the most common choking hazards?

Balls that are just small enough to lodge in the throat (a very common cause in medium and large dogs), cooked bones, rawhide chunks, corn cobs, plastic fragments and toy squeakers. For cats, string and hair ties are the classic hazard — and with those, never pull.

Should I take a real first aid class?

If you can, yes — reading is not the same as having done it. Many veterinary clinics, the Red Cross and pet-care organisations run short pet first aid courses, and hands-on practice on a mannequin is what makes the movements automatic when you are frightened.
