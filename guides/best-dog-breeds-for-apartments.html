The 10 Best Dog Breeds for Apartments

Here's a truth the dog world doesn't say out loud enough: you don't need a backyard to give a dog a wonderful life. You need the right dog — and a little know-how. Some of the happiest dogs on earth live in 500 square feet with a human who adores them.

But pick the wrong breed for apartment life and you'll both feel it — the 6 a.m. zoomies, the barking that makes you dread the neighbors, the restlessness no amount of love can fix. So before you fall for a cute face, let's talk about what actually makes a dog thrive in a small space — including the one breed on this list that surprises almost everyone.

Forget size — this is what really matters

The biggest myth in apartment dog ownership is that small = suitable. It's not that simple. A hyper little terrier can be far harder in a flat than a calm giant. Four things matter more than the number on the scale:

  • Energy level — can the dog switch off indoors, or does it need a job all day?
  • Barking tendency — thin walls are unforgiving; quiet breeds keep the peace (and your lease).
  • Adaptability & people-focus — apartment dogs are with you a lot. They should love that.
  • Tolerance for alone time — some breeds nap happily; others spiral the moment you leave.

Keep those four in mind and this list makes perfect sense.

The 10 best dog breeds for apartments

1. French Bulldog

Small 16–28 lb · Energy: low–moderate · Barking: low · Grooming: low

The unofficial mascot of apartment living. Frenchies are basically furry roommates who exist to nap on you, snort adorably, and greet you like a returning war hero. Low exercise needs, rarely yappy. Watch-out: as a flat-faced breed they overheat fast and can have breathing issues — keep them cool and skip strenuous heat-of-day walks.

2. Cavalier King Charles Spaniel

Small 13–18 lb · Energy: moderate · Barking: low · Grooming: moderate

Velcro — in the best possible way. The Cavalier wants to be wherever you are, which is exactly the temperament a small space rewards. Gentle, quiet, endlessly cuddly. Watch-out: they hate being alone all day, and the breed is prone to heart issues — buy only from health-testing breeders.

3. Bichon Frise

Small 12–18 lb · Energy: moderate · Barking: moderate · Grooming: high

A cotton ball with a comedian's soul — cheerful, friendly, and famously low-shedding, which makes the Bichon a favorite for allergy-prone apartment dwellers. Watch-out: they need regular grooming and can get vocal or anxious if left bored and alone too long.

4. Pug

Small 14–18 lb · Energy: low · Barking: low · Grooming: low

Comedy in a compact package. Pugs are content to lounge, love everyone they meet, and ask for very little exercise — perfect for a cozy home. Watch-out: another flat-faced breed (mind the heat), and they gain weight easily, so keep the treats honest.

5. Boston Terrier

Small 12–25 lb · Energy: moderate · Barking: low–moderate · Grooming: low

The dapper "American Gentleman." Friendly, adaptable and relatively quiet, with just enough energy to be fun and not enough to wreck your living room. Watch-out: flat-faced (heat sensitive) and prone to the occasional burst of zoomies that a quick play session solves.

Now for the plot twist… If someone told you the calmest dog in the building is a former racing athlete, you'd laugh. Keep reading.

6. Greyhound

Large 60–70 lb · Energy: low (indoors!) · Barking: very low · Grooming: low

The 45-mph couch potato. Yes — a Greyhound is a giant, and yes, it's one of the best apartment dogs alive. Sprinters by design, they're champion nappers by lifestyle: quiet, gentle, and content to sleep 18 hours a day after a couple of good runs. The surprise of this whole list. Watch-out: they need those daily sprints, feel the cold (thin coat — get a sweater), and many are sensitive souls.

7. Shih Tzu

Small 9–16 lb · Energy: low · Barking: low · Grooming: high

Bred centuries ago to be a palace lapdog — a job they still take very seriously. Affectionate, mellow, and happy with short strolls and long cuddles. Watch-out: that gorgeous coat needs real grooming, and the flat face means a little heat caution.

8. Maltese

Small 4–7 lb · Energy: low–moderate · Barking: moderate · Grooming: high

Tiny, devoted and low-shedding — a Maltese folds into apartment life beautifully and adores being a constant companion. Watch-out: they can become yappy or anxious without gentle training, and at this size they're delicate around very young kids.

9. Havanese

Small 7–13 lb · Energy: moderate · Barking: low–moderate · Grooming: high

Cuba's velcro clown — bright, cheerful and so people-focused they're practically a shadow. Low-shedding and highly adaptable, they're built for life beside you. Watch-out: they crave company (not a leave-all-day dog) and the silky coat needs upkeep.

10. Dachshund

Small 11–32 lb · Energy: moderate · Barking: moderate–high · Grooming: low–moderate

Big-dog personality in a small-dog footprint — loyal, funny and endlessly entertaining. Moderate needs make them flat-friendly. Watch-out: they're alert barkers (train it early for the neighbors' sake), stubborn, and that long back means no jumping off high furniture — protect those spines.

The breeds to think twice about

Gorgeous dogs, wrong setting. These tend to struggle in apartments — usually because of energy, noise, or a deep need for a job:

  • High-drive working dogs (Border Collie, Australian Shepherd, Belgian Malinois) — brilliant, tireless, and miserable without hours of work.
  • Vocal hounds & terriers (Beagle, Jack Russell) — bred to bay and dig; your neighbors will know.
  • Northern breeds (Husky, Malamute) — athletes that shed cities of fur and howl the building awake.

None are "bad" — they just need space, jobs and tolerant neighbors most apartments can't offer.

How to make any apartment a dog's happy place

Honestly, the right routine matters as much as the right breed. Five things turn a small space into a content dog's whole world:

  1. Walk it like you mean it. Two real, sniff-everything walks a day beat one rushed lap. A tired dog is a quiet dog.
  2. Feed the brain. Lick mats, puzzle feeders and 5-minute training games burn more energy than you'd think.
  3. Build a potty plan. A consistent schedule (or a balcony pad as backup) saves everyone's sanity.
  4. Manage the barking early. Reward calm, desensitize hallway sounds — your lease and your neighbors will thank you.
  5. Give it a "den." A cozy bed or crate in a quiet corner gives a small-space dog a place that's all its own.

One last thing before you fall in love

Two reality checks save a lot of heartbreak. First, small doesn't mean cheap — food, vet care and grooming add up over 12–15 years; our pet cost calculator shows the real number. Second, apartment dogs are indoor dogs, which means the snacks you share matter even more — keep chocolate, grapes and other hazards out of reach, and check anything new in seconds with our free food checker or the full list of foods toxic to dogs.

The alone-time reality check

This is the part most "best apartment dog" lists skip. Dogs are social animals, and separation-related problems — barking, howling, destructive chewing, house-soiling — are among the most common reasons people give up on pets. Apartments make the fallout louder, because your neighbors hear everything.

  • Build up gradually. Start with a few minutes alone and extend slowly, rewarding calm behavior. Don't make departures dramatic.
  • Exercise first, then leave. A sniff-heavy walk before you go helps a dog settle. Our dog exercise guide explains how much each type of dog needs.
  • Leave something to do. A stuffed toy or lick mat gives a dog a job. Try a few DIY dog toys if you'd rather not buy new ones.
  • Plan for midday. Puppies and seniors cannot hold it all day. A walker, neighbor or daycare visit is part of the real cost — see our guide to how much time a dog needs.
  • Get help early. If a dog panics when left (frantic escape attempts, drooling, non-stop howling), talk to your vet or a certified behavior professional rather than waiting it out.

Getting a puppy? The first months need a lot of presence. Our puppy daily schedule and new puppy checklist walk through what a realistic day looks like.

Renting? Lease, landlord and neighbor basics

Many buildings have pet rules, and they are often about more than whether a dog is allowed. Before you fall for a specific breed, read your lease and ask the landlord or building manager about:

  • Weight or breed restrictions. Some buildings cap weight, and some insurers exclude certain breeds. A Greyhound may need a conversation that a Pug doesn't.
  • Pet deposits, monthly pet rent and number of pets. These add to the real cost of apartment life, so budget for them with the pet cost calculator.
  • Noise clauses. Persistent barking is a common reason for lease complaints, which is why the barking column above matters.
  • Common-area rules. Elevators, hallways and lobbies often require a short leash, and some buildings designate specific relief areas.
  • Assistance animals. Service and assistance animals are generally treated differently from pets under housing law, but the rules vary by location, so check local requirements.

Get any agreement about your dog in writing, and keep your vaccination records handy — see our vaccine schedule tool.

Apartment safety: the hazards small spaces hide

  • Balconies. Check railing gaps and never leave a dog unattended on a balcony — small dogs can squeeze through bars and excited dogs can jump.
  • Open windows and screens. Window screens are built to keep bugs out, not to hold an 18-lb dog leaning on them.
  • Houseplants. Some popular plants, including sago palm and lilies, are dangerous to pets. Check the ASPCA's toxic plant list before bringing one home.
  • Cleaning products and pest control. Store them up high and ask building management which pest treatments are used in shared spaces.
  • Dropped human food. In a small kitchen, a dog is always within reach of the counter. Chocolate, grapes and anything sweetened with xylitol are the big ones.
If your dog eats something it shouldn't: don't wait for symptoms. Use our pet emergency tool and call your vet, an emergency clinic, or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center or Pet Poison Helpline right away.

Healthy treats for small-space dogs

Dogs who spend most of their time indoors burn fewer calories, so treats add up fast, especially in toy breeds. A common vet guideline is to keep treats to about 10% of daily calories, and our dog feeding calculator helps you work out the rest. Low-calorie options that are generally safe in plain, small portions include carrots, green beans and blueberries. Check your dog's shape with the body condition score tool, especially for easy gainers like Pugs.

Trick-or-treaters mean a constant parade of doorbell rings and strangers, which is stressful for many apartment dogs. Keep your dog in a quiet room with a chew or lick mat, and keep candy well out of reach. Our Halloween pet safety guide covers the hazards in detail. Frequently asked questions

What is the best dog for a small apartment?

French Bulldogs, Cavalier King Charles Spaniels, Pugs and Shih Tzus are common top picks because they have low to moderate exercise needs and a mellow indoor temperament. The best match also depends on your schedule, noise tolerance and grooming appetite.

Can a big dog live in an apartment?

Yes, if its energy level fits. Greyhounds are the classic example: large but calm indoors, as long as they get regular exercise. A high-energy large breed, such as a Labrador or a herding breed, is a much harder fit for a small space.

What is the quietest apartment dog breed?

Greyhounds are known for being very quiet. French Bulldogs, Pugs and Cavaliers also tend to bark little. Barking varies by individual and depends heavily on exercise, training and how much time the dog spends alone.

How much exercise does an apartment dog need?

It depends on the dog, but most adult dogs benefit from at least a couple of walks a day plus some play and mental work. Even low-energy breeds need daily activity to stay healthy and calm. See our dog exercise guide for a breakdown by age and type.

Is it cruel to keep a dog in an apartment?

No. A dog's wellbeing depends on exercise, company, mental stimulation and care, not square footage. A well-matched dog with an attentive owner can thrive in a small space, while a bored, lonely dog can be unhappy in a big house.

How long can I leave my dog alone in an apartment?

Many adult dogs can manage a few hours, but it varies with age, training and temperament. Puppies and senior dogs need more frequent breaks. If you work long hours, plan for a midday walker or daycare.

Are small dogs always better for apartments?

No. Some small breeds are loud, high-energy or hard to house-train. Temperament, barking tendency and exercise needs matter more than weight.

Should I get a puppy or an adult dog for an apartment?

Adult dogs, including retired racing Greyhounds, often arrive with settled temperaments and house manners, which can make apartment life easier. Puppies need frequent potty trips, constant supervision and a lot of training, so they suit people with flexible schedules.
