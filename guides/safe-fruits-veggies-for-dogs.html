Healthy Treats Guide

Safe Fruits & Veggies Dogs Can Eat

Updated 17 September 2026 — expanded to 25 picks, with a portion chart by dog weight and an autumn produce section.

Good news — plenty of fruits and vegetables make healthy, low-calorie treats for dogs, and several are favorites with vets for everything from weight loss to settling an upset stomach. Here are 25 safe picks, what each one is actually good for, how much to give, and how to serve it right.

🐾 The golden rule: even safe foods are treats — keep them to about 10% of your dog's daily calories, serve them plain (no salt, sugar, butter or seasoning), remove pits/seeds/cores, cut into bite-size pieces, and introduce any new food slowly.
🍂 In season right now: autumn is the easiest time of year to share produce with a dog. Pumpkin, butternut squash, apples, pears, sweet potato and fresh cranberries are all on this list and all in the shops — just remember that the cooked, spiced versions of most of them (pie filling, candied yams, sauces) are not dog food. Apple Fiber + Vitamin C

A crunchy, hydrating treat that's about 85% water. Remove the core and seeds (the seeds have trace cyanide).

Soft, sweet and full of fiber and copper. Treat it like an apple: core it, and take out every seed before serving.

Cantaloupe Hydrating · Vitamin A

Like watermelon's richer cousin — lots of water and beta-carotene, but noticeably more sugar, so it's a small-portion treat (especially for overweight or diabetic dogs).

Raspberries Low-sugar · Anti-inflammatory

One of the lowest-sugar fruits you can share, and a nice option for older dogs with stiff joints. They do contain trace natural xylitol, so the "small handful" rule matters here more than usual.

Cranberries In moderation

Plain fresh or dried cranberries are safe in small amounts. The catch is what they usually come in — cranberry sauce, juice blends and trail mix are loaded with sugar, and some dried mixes contain raisins, which are toxic.

Serve: a few plain berries; skip sauce entirely.

Nutrient-packed, but high in oxalates — fine as an occasional garnish, not a daily side, and worth avoiding altogether in dogs with kidney or urinary-stone history. Ask your vet first if that's your dog.

🔪 Prep matters more than you'd think

Most produce problems we hear about aren't toxicity — they're preparation. A few habits cover almost all of it:

  • Cut for the mouth you're feeding. A whole baby carrot is a snack for a Labrador and a choking hazard for a French Bulldog. Size pieces to your dog, not the recipe.
  • Pits, cores and stones come out first. Cherry, peach, plum and mango pits are the classic emergency-room story — they block, and the pits themselves contain cyanide compounds.
  • Plain means plain. No salt, butter, oil, garlic or onion powder. Onion and garlic are toxic to dogs, and they hide in almost every savoury seasoning blend.
  • Wash it. Pesticide residue and dirt aren't doing your dog any favors, and grocery-store produce isn't washed for you.
  • One new food at a time. Introduce a single item, wait a day or two, and watch the stool. If something disagrees, you'll know exactly what.
  • Frozen is a feature. Frozen blueberries, carrot sticks and watermelon chunks last longer, cost nothing extra, and are genuinely good for teething puppies and hot afternoons.

🚫 Produce to skip

Some of the most dangerous things a dog can eat come from the same fridge as everything above. These are the produce-aisle items to keep firmly out of reach:

  • Grapes
  • Raisins
  • Onion
  • Garlic
  • Avocado
  • Cherries (pits)
  • Green tomato & leaves
  • Macadamia nuts

Grapes and raisins deserve a special mention: there is no known safe dose, the reaction varies wildly between dogs, and even a few can cause acute kidney failure. If your dog has eaten any, that's a call-the-vet-now situation, not a wait-and-see one — our grape & raisin calculator can help you describe the amount while you're on the phone.

⚠️ Not everything in the produce aisle is safe. Grapes, raisins, onions and a few others are genuinely dangerous — see the full list of foods toxic to dogs before you share anything new, or check any food in seconds.

Frequently asked questions

Can dogs eat fruit and veggies every day?

In small amounts, yes — but treats (including produce) should stay around 10% of daily calories. The rest should be complete, balanced dog food.

Raw or cooked — which is better?

Both work. Some (sweet potato, pumpkin) should be cooked plain; others (carrot, cucumber) are great raw. Always skip salt, butter, oil and seasoning.

Do I need to remove seeds and pits?

Yes — remove apple cores/seeds, mango/cherry pits and watermelon seeds. They're choking or toxicity risks.

How do I work out the right treat amount?

Use our calorie calculator for your dog's daily target, then keep treats under 10% of it — see the portion chart above for a rough starting point by weight.

Can puppies have fruit and vegetables?

Yes, in smaller amounts — a puppy's treat budget is tiny, and their growing bodies need the balanced nutrition in puppy food far more than they need extras. Start with soft, low-sugar options (a green bean, a blueberry, a frozen carrot for teething) and keep it rare.

What about juice, smoothies or dried fruit?

Skip them. Juice and smoothies concentrate the sugar and lose the fiber, dried fruit concentrates the sugar even further, and both often contain grapes, raisins or xylitol. Fresh, plain and whole is always the safer version.

My dog has diabetes or is overweight — which picks are best?

Lean toward the low-sugar, high-water options: green beans, cucumber, zucchini, lettuce, celery and raspberries. Go easy on banana, mango, cantaloupe and pineapple. Any dog with a diagnosed condition should have their treat plan cleared with their own vet first.

Can vegetables replace part of my dog's meal?

Not on your own. Swapping in vegetables to cut calories is something vets do prescribe — but it's done with a plan, because unbalancing a complete diet causes its own problems. Ask your vet before making produce a meal component rather than a treat.

My dog ate something from this list and seems unwell. Now what?

Mild gas or a soft stool after a new food is usually just too much, too fast. Repeated vomiting, lethargy, or any suspicion your dog ate something on the "skip" list means calling your vet or an emergency line right away — start with our pet emergency guide.
