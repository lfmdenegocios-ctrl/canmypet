10 Things Dogs Secretly Love (and 5 They Quietly Hate)

Dogs can't tell us what makes their day, so we tend to guess — and some of our best-intentioned guesses are wrong. A full-on hug can stress a dog out more than it comforts them, while a boring five extra minutes of sniffing the same fence post might be the highlight of their whole walk. Here's what vets, canine behaviorists, and actual research say dogs genuinely love, and the handful of common habits they quietly tolerate rather than enjoy.

The short version: Dogs love things that let them use their nose, choose for themselves, and predict what's coming next — sniffing, routine, gentle touch in the right spots, and your scent. They're less into things that trap them physically or socially, like tight hugs, forced eye contact, or being looked over from above. None of this is universal — every dog is an individual — but it's a solid, evidence-backed starting point.

10 things dogs secretly love

1. Sniffing — really, really sniffing Enrichment

A sniff isn't just a sniff. It's how dogs gather intel on who's been by, what's nearby, and how another animal might be feeling — something like scrolling the neighborhood news. Letting your dog linger at an interesting smell instead of tugging them along isn't wasted walk time; behaviorists increasingly treat it as one of the simplest, free forms of mental enrichment available. See our fun games to play with your dog guide for more low-effort ways to work their nose and brain.

2. A predictable routine Comfort

Dogs don't wear watches, but they clearly track time and pattern. Consistent mealtimes, walk windows, and bedtime rituals let a dog anticipate what's coming next, which lowers everyday stress — especially for anxious, senior, or newly adopted dogs still learning the rhythm of a home.

3. Chin, chest, and base-of-tail scratches Touch

Belly rubs get all the attention, but they're not actually every dog's favorite — a dog rolling onto its back can be asking for a rub, or it can be a stress or appeasement signal. Under the chin, on the chest, and near the base of the tail are safer, broadly loved spots most dogs lean into.

4. Your worn, unwashed t-shirt Scent

A dog's sense of smell is built to key in on the people it's bonded to. An item that smells like you — a worn shirt in the crate, a pillow on the couch — can be genuinely comforting during separations, vet visits, or a new environment.

5. Licking and chewing as a way to self-soothe Calming

Trainers sometimes call sniffing, licking, and chewing the "trifecta of calm" — all three activate a relaxation response in dogs. A lick mat, a safe chew, or a stuffed puzzle toy filled with plain peanut butter or pumpkin gives that instinct somewhere healthy to go, instead of your furniture.

6. Physical contact — on their terms Touch

Given the choice between a treat and calm petting from their favorite person, many dogs pick the person. Touch releases oxytocin and lowers stress hormones in both of you within minutes — as long as it's contact the dog chose, not contact they're stuck with.

7. Praise paired with something they already like Bonding

A cheerful "good boy!" on its own does very little for most dogs — but the same words paired with a pet, a treat, or a game become genuinely rewarding over time. It's the tone and the pairing that build the meaning, not the words themselves.

8. Choices, even small ones Agency

Picking which direction to walk, which toy to play with, or how long to sniff a bush might sound trivial, but behaviorists point to a sense of control as a real driver of a dog's confidence and wellbeing — the opposite of constantly being steered.

9. A job to do Enrichment

Many breeds were bred to work, and a surprising number of behavior issues trace back to a dog with energy and no outlet. Puzzle feeders, scent games, basic training sessions, or a simple "find it" game around the house all scratch that itch.

10. Being near you — not necessarily doing anything Bonding

Dogs are a famously social species, and plenty of research points to proximity to their person as calming in itself, separate from active play or petting. A dog snoozing at your feet while you work isn't just being lazy — that's the good part of their day.

5 things dogs quietly hate (or just tolerate)

1. Tight, full-body hugs Stress

A well-known analysis of photos of people hugging dogs found the large majority showed stress signals — turned-away heads, flattened ears, lip-licking, or the whites of their eyes showing. Dogs generally prefer contact that lets all four paws stay on the ground.

2. Direct, unbroken eye contact Discomfort

A quick, soft glance is fine, but a hard, sustained stare can read as a challenge in dog body language, especially from a stranger. If a dog looks away, blinks, or turns its head during eye contact, that's a polite request for space — see our dog body language guide for the full list of signals.

3. Being loomed over or patted on the head Discomfort

Reaching over a dog's head from above, especially a dog that doesn't know you, can feel confrontational rather than friendly. Letting a dog approach you, and scratching under the chin instead of patting the top of the head, tends to land much better.

4. Sudden, repeated changes to routine Stress

One schedule shake-up isn't a big deal, but frequent, unpredictable changes to mealtimes, walks, or who's home when can chip away at a dog's sense of security over time — particularly for dogs already prone to anxiety.

5. Being rushed past something they want to sniff Frustration

It circles back to #1 on the love list: constantly yanking a dog away from an interesting smell, over and over on every walk, removes one of their few chances at self-directed enrichment. It's a small thing that adds up.

Every dog is an individual. These are strong general patterns from behaviorists and researchers, not rules for every single dog — plenty of dogs genuinely love hugs from their own person, and some skip belly rubs entirely. Watch your own dog's body language rather than assuming. Our dog body language guide is a good place to start reading the signals for yourself.

Frequently asked questions

Do dogs really not like being hugged?

Research analyzing photos of dogs being hugged found the large majority showed stress signals — turned heads, flattened ears, lip-licking, or whale eye. Some individual dogs tolerate or even enjoy hugs from people they trust, but as a species, dogs are more comfortable with contact that lets all four paws stay on the ground.

Why do dogs love sniffing on walks so much?

Sniffing is how dogs gather information about their world — other animals, food, territory, even how another dog is feeling. It's mentally engaging and calming, similar to how a person might feel scrolling the news. Letting your dog stop and sniff isn't wasted walk time, it's enrichment.

Do dogs actually like belly rubs?

Some do, some don't. A dog rolling onto its back can be a request for a belly rub, but it can also be an appeasement or stress signal. Chest, chin, and the base of the tail are safer bets that most dogs enjoy, while a belly should only be rubbed if the dog is clearly loose and relaxed.

Is it bad to change my dog's routine?

Occasional change is fine and even good for adaptability, but dogs generally find predictable mealtimes, walks, and routines comforting because it lets them anticipate what's next. Big, sudden, repeated changes can raise stress, especially in anxious or older dogs.
