How Much Exercise Does My Dog Need?

Updated 28 September 2026 · Reviewed against AKC, AAHA Canine Life Stage Guidelines & veterinary rehabilitation sources

"Enough exercise" looks completely different for a senior Chihuahua than it does for a young Border Collie — and getting it wrong in either direction causes real problems, from destructive chewing and weight gain to joint strain and exhaustion. Here's a source-checked breakdown of how much daily activity dogs actually need by age, breed, and health status, a quick-reference table, and the signs your dog isn't getting enough (or is getting too much).

The short answer: Most healthy adult dogs need 30–120 minutes of physical activity a day, split across walks and play. Puppies need frequent short bursts (not long runs), seniors need gentler, more frequent movement, and high-energy working breeds often need well over an hour of real exertion to stay content. When in doubt, match the amount to your individual dog's age, breed, and health — not a single fixed number.

Here's the short version before the details. Treat these as starting ranges, not a rulebook — your dog's individual health and energy level still matter more than any chart.

How much exercise by life stage

Puppies (0–12 months) Short & frequent

Puppies have plenty of energy but immature joints, so the rule of thumb many vets and canine rehab specialists use is roughly 5 minutes of structured exercise per month of age, twice a day, until they're fully grown (e.g., a 4-month-old puppy gets about 20 minutes, twice daily). Free play in a yard and mental games count too and are gentler on growing bones than repetitive running or jumping. Growth plates — the soft cartilage areas near the ends of a puppy's bones — typically stay open until roughly 12–18 months, later in large and giant breeds like Great Danes and Mastiffs, so hard running, jumping off furniture, and long hikes are best saved until then.

Adult dogs (1–7 years) 30–120 min/day

Most adult dogs do well with 30 minutes to 2 hours of combined walking, play, and training per day, depending on breed and energy level. Splitting it into two sessions (morning and evening) tends to work better than one long outing, and mixing in mental enrichment — sniffing walks, puzzle feeders, training — helps satisfy dogs who tire out mentally as much as physically.

Senior dogs (7+ years, earlier for large breeds) Gentle & consistent

Older dogs still need regular movement — it helps maintain muscle, joint mobility, and a healthy weight — but shorter, more frequent, lower-impact sessions usually beat one long walk. Swimming and gentle leash walks are easier on arthritic joints than running or jumping. If your senior dog seems to be slowing down suddenly, it's worth a vet visit rather than assuming it's "just age."

It also depends on the breed and energy level

Age is only half the picture — a dog's breed background matters just as much:

High-energy & working breeds 60–120+ min/day

Border Collies, Australian Shepherds, Huskies, Vizslas, and other herding or sporting breeds were bred to work all day. Without a real outlet — running, fetch, agility, structured play — that energy often turns into barking, digging, or destructive chewing at home.

Low-energy & companion breeds 20–40 min/day

Bulldogs, Basset Hounds, Cavalier King Charles Spaniels, and other laid-back breeds are usually content with shorter, gentler walks — and brachycephalic (short-nosed) breeds need extra care to avoid overheating during any exertion. See our calmest, low-energy dog breeds guide for a fuller list if you're choosing a dog to match a quieter lifestyle.

Does spaying/neutering or a health condition change the number?

Spaying or neutering doesn't reduce a dog's need for daily movement, but metabolism can shift slightly afterward — part of why some dogs gain weight if food portions aren't adjusted to match activity. Keeping up regular exercise and tracking your dog's body condition score matters more than assuming a fixed dog automatically needs less exercise.

Dogs with arthritis, heart disease, or brachycephalic airways often still benefit from daily movement — just shorter, gentler, and more frequent rather than one long session. Ask your vet for a tailored plan, and use our calorie calculator to help match food to whatever activity level your dog actually gets.

Mental exercise counts just as much as physical

A tired dog isn't only a dog who ran a lot — it's often a dog whose brain got a workout too. Sniffing, problem-solving, and training tap into instincts a straight-line walk doesn't, and many trainers find a slow, sniff-heavy walk leaves a dog just as settled as a longer walk at a brisk pace.

  • Sniffing walks: let your dog set the pace and investigate — it's mentally tiring in a good way and costs nothing extra.
  • Short training sessions (5–10 minutes): recall, "leave it," or a new trick, rewarded with a small plain cooked chicken or peanut butter treat you've already checked is safe.
  • Puzzle feeders and a DIY puzzle toy for lower-key or rainy days.
  • Scent games — hiding treats or a favorite toy around a room and letting your dog find them.

Signs your dog isn't getting enough exercise

  • Destructive behavior — chewing furniture, digging, or shredding things that were fine last month.
  • Restlessness or hyperactivity, especially in the evening ("zoomies" that seem to come from nowhere).
  • Excessive barking or whining for attention or stimulation.
  • Weight gain without a change in food portions.
  • Difficulty settling or constant pacing indoors.

Signs of too much exercise — or unsafe exercise

More isn't always better, especially for puppies, seniors, and dogs in hot weather. Watch for:

  • Heavy panting that doesn't ease up after a rest, or panting that sounds raspy.
  • Lagging behind, sitting down mid-walk, or reluctance to continue.
  • Limping or stiffness, especially the day after activity.
  • Excessive thirst or exhaustion that lasts well beyond the activity itself.
  • Vomiting or collapse — both are emergencies. If your dog collapses or seems disoriented after exercise, especially on a hot day, treat it as urgent: contact your vet or use our "my pet ate something / emergency" helper to find your next step right away.

Exercise safety by weather

Heat: dogs cool themselves mainly by panting, which is far less efficient than human sweating — and brachycephalic (flat-faced) breeds like Bulldogs, Pugs, and Boxers have a harder time moving air through their shortened airways, putting them at meaningfully higher heatstroke risk even on moderately warm days. Exercise early morning or after sunset in summer, and check pavement with the back-of-hand test (7 seconds) before a midday walk — our hot pavement & walk safety checker gives a quick read.

Cold: short-haired, small, senior, and very young dogs lose body heat fast and may need a coat for anything beyond a quick bathroom break in freezing weather. Watch paws for ice buildup between toes, and rinse them after walks on salted sidewalks — rock salt and some de-icers can irritate skin or cause stomach upset if licked off.

Did you know? A dog's normal body temperature (roughly 101–102.5°F) already runs higher than ours, so a day that feels mild to you can be warmer than it seems for your dog — especially one carrying extra weight or a heavy coat.

🍂 Fall exercise safety

Cooler temperatures make autumn one of the best seasons for longer, more active outings — with a few seasonal things to watch for:

  • Shorter days mean darker walks. As sunset creeps earlier, a reflective leash, collar, or vest helps drivers and cyclists spot your dog on evening walks.
  • Fallen nuts, acorns, and wild mushrooms can cause stomach upset or worse if eaten — keep an eye on what your dog is sniffing and swallowing in piles of leaves.
  • Moldy, decaying leaf piles can harbor mycotoxins that cause tremors if eaten; it's one of the lesser-known entries on our foods (and non-foods) toxic to dogs guide.
  • Halloween candy bowls left at dog height are a real emergency-vet trend every October. If your dog gets into chocolate, xylitol gum, or raisins, use our "my pet ate something" helper right away.
  • Pumpkin itself is a safe, seasonal treat in small, plain, cooked amounts — see can dogs eat pumpkin? for portions, and a good pairing for a post-walk training session.

Easy ways to fit exercise into a normal week

You don't need a big yard or hours of free time — consistency matters more than intensity. A few low-effort ways to add activity:

  • Turn a walk into a sniffing walk — let your dog set the pace and explore; it's mentally tiring in a good way.
  • Add short training sessions (5–10 minutes) — mental work burns energy too, and it's a great option on rainy days when you'd otherwise skip activity entirely.
  • Rotate in a few of our fun games to play with your dog for active days, and a DIY puzzle toy for lower-key enrichment when you're short on time.
  • Use stairs, fetch in a hallway, or a flirt pole for quick bursts of activity indoors when weather doesn't cooperate.
  • On multi-dog or multi-pet walks, exercise dogs separately at least once a week if energy levels differ a lot — a high-energy dog can otherwise drag a lower-energy housemate further than is comfortable for them.

Frequently asked questions

Is a daily walk enough exercise for my dog?

For many low- to moderate-energy adult dogs, one or two solid walks plus some mental enrichment is enough. High-energy and working breeds usually need more — added running, fetch, or structured playtime on top of walks.

Can a dog get too much exercise?

Yes. Puppies especially can strain growing joints with too much high-impact activity, and dogs of any age can overheat or overexert on hot days. Build up any new activity gradually and watch for excessive panting, lagging behind, or limping.

What if my dog can't handle much exercise due to a health issue?

Talk to your vet about a safe activity level. Short, frequent, low-impact sessions (gentle walks, swimming) are often better than one long outing for dogs with joint, heart, or breathing conditions.

How do I know if it's too hot to walk my dog?

If pavement is too hot for the back of your hand for 7 seconds, it's too hot for paws. Our hot pavement checker gives a quick read based on conditions.

Does spaying or neutering change how much exercise my dog needs?

Not directly — spaying and neutering don't reduce a dog's need for daily movement. Metabolism can shift slightly afterward, though, which is part of why some dogs gain weight if food portions aren't adjusted. Keep up regular exercise and track your dog's body condition rather than assuming a fixed dog needs less activity.

Should my dog take rest days, or is daily exercise fine?

Daily activity is fine and often ideal for most healthy adult dogs. What helps is mixing intensity — alternating a high-energy day (running, fetch, agility) with a lower-key one (a sniffing walk, short stroll) to avoid overuse injuries, especially for active breeds doing repetitive, high-impact activity.

What's the best time of day to exercise my dog in hot weather?

Early morning or after sunset, when air and pavement temperatures are lowest. Check pavement with the back-of-hand test before a midday walk, and keep an especially close eye on brachycephalic (flat-faced) breeds like Bulldogs and Pugs, whose airway shape makes it harder for them to cool down by panting.

Can indoor games replace a walk on a bad-weather day?

Occasionally, yes. A vigorous indoor session — a flirt pole, stairs, fetch down a hallway, or a few training games — plus some puzzle-feeder time can substitute for a walk now and then. It shouldn't replace regular outdoor time long-term, though, since dogs also benefit from the sensory variety and sniffing opportunities that being outside provides.
